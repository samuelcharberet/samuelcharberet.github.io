Can life be defined? A critical reading of Tirard, Morange and Lazcano (2010)

Created in November 24, 2026

2026   ·   definition-of-life   review   ·   reviews

In the opening chapter of her recent book Alive (2026, the year I am writing this post), Melanie Challenger rightly points out that “we still lack a generally agreed upon definition of life”, citing the 2010 essay by Stéphane Tirard, Michel Morange and Antonio Lazcano, The definition of life: a brief history of an elusive scientific endeavor, published in Astrobiology. She quotes them again: “this is not for want of trying”. My curiosity was piqued; I am always excited by these questions. However, I was disappointed while reading the paper by what I see as a dogmatic view of life as almost by nature undefinable. Recently, on the contrary, I have had the impression that life only exists in some places, that these places can be defined in terms of a few relevant parameters and not others, and that these parameters could therefore become a suitable definition, or circumscription, of life. Physics might have something to say about it. Below are some of my thoughts on this paper, which you can find here:

Tirard, S., Morange, M. and Lazcano, A. (2010). The definition of life: a brief history of an elusive scientific endeavor. Astrobiology 10(10): 1003–1009. https://doi.org/10.1089/ast.2010.0535

The essay in a few words

The argument goes roughly as follows. Despite all the progress of molecular biology, there is still no generally agreed-upon definition of life. Naturalists from Buffon to Darwin, Oparin and Haldane worked on the origin of life with conceptions of life, never with explicit definitions. The authors then go through the main candidate parameters, or criteria, that have historically been used, or at least considered relevant, to define life: reproduction and mutation (Muller), self-maintenance and autopoiesis (Bernal, Maturana and Varela, Gánti), physical and complexity-based approaches (Schrödinger, self-organisation), and finally Darwinian evolution. None of them is satisfactory on its own, and I agree with that. However, the authors conclude that life is an empirical concept, that a precise definition will remain “at best, a work in progress”, and that the real question is now historical: not what distinguishes organisms from inanimate objects, but how these characteristics came to be associated.

“Empirical concepts cannot be defined”, or the idea that life is holy

I am not trained as a philosopher, and I probably do not know or understand all the nuances of the definition of “definition” itself. However, the way I understand the problem, life is a set of observations that still lacks a universal model. Very early on, the authors invoke Kant and Nietzsche: precise definitions are possible in mathematics and philosophy, but empirical concepts, like life, can only be described in a historical context. Some concepts “only have a history” and cannot be defined. This sounds dogmatic to me. Atoms have a very long history in science, dating back to ancient Greece. For most of that history they lacked a proper definition, because nobody had ever seen, measured or understood an atom. More recently, however, physicists arrived at a satisfactory circumscription of what the word atom applies to, along with mathematical descriptions of atoms. Why would life be fundamentally different, a priori?

The idea that life is fundamentally undefinable and not amenable to a universal model, whether because of its high complexity, its irreducibility to physical laws alone, emergence, or on philosophical grounds, is quite common in the life sciences, and is apparently also shared by historians of science. I do not see why life should be so hard to understand that it would escape a universal understanding forever. Recently I have come to realise that biologists and physicists do not always get along very well. Physicists put the emphasis on invariants and universal laws, while biologists like to explore diversity, which is somewhat the opposite of universality. I was trained in both fields, and I do not see a contradiction here. But I digress. Tirard and colleagues seem to base the undefinability of life on grounds that are more philosophical than complexity or emergence. However, they do not really explain this satisfactorily.

The tone of the conclusion goes along the same lines: “research in the origin and nature of life is doomed to remain, at best, as a work in progress.” Quite frankly, I almost read it as: life is too special to be defined, so do not try too hard. This is not a very scientific attitude.

Two different problems

My main issue with this paper is what I see as a confusion between two different problems: the definition of life and the explanation of its origin. To be fair, the authors cite a lot of Morange’s work (which I have not read) on the link between these two questions, which might show that they are equivalent. I certainly believe they are related, but I do not see how the question of the nature of life could suddenly collapse into, and become identical to, the question of its origin.

Take planets. They are fairly easy to define: celestial objects with a particular location, motion and trajectory around a star, a particular composition, and so on. Explaining how planets formed is a completely different problem, with its own models and data. Nobody would say that we cannot define a planet because we do not fully understand planet formation. For life, the essay does exactly that. A lot of the discussion is about the RNA world, whether metabolism came before genes, and whether self-organising chemical networks could have started without genetic polymers. These are fascinating questions, but they are questions about the origin, not about the definition.

The same confusion appears when they discuss self-organisation:

“It is easy to appreciate the appeal of autopoiesis and complexity theory when attempting to understand the basic nature of living systems. The sophisticated theoretical models derived from the perspective of self-organization theories have not been substantiated by unambiguous empirical evidence demonstrating that a system of large or small molecules can spontaneously arise and evolve into non-genetic catalytic networks.” (Tirard et al., 2010, p. 1008)

Note that the first sentence, about the appeal of autopoiesis and complexity theory, is a statement about the nature of living systems, while the second, about the lack of evidence that non-genetic catalytic networks can arise spontaneously, is a statement about their origin. In my opinion, these are two different problems. Theoretical models of self-organisation aim at describing how life started, so they also need a definition of life to begin with. The question of the origin is therefore a harder problem than the definition alone. This means that the first and easier step is to define, or circumscribe, life! Only then can the problem of the origin be tackled in a well-defined manner (which does not mean that we should stop researching the origin of life until we have a definition of it).

Ideas dominated by history and contingency

Darwinian evolution is the criterion the authors care most about. They put heavy emphasis on the history of species as part of any attempt to define life. For instance:

“From the viewpoint of contemporary biology, a proper understanding of the minimal properties required for a system to be considered alive requires the recognition of the evolutionary processes that led to the system itself.” (Tirard et al., 2010, p. 1005)

“it is somewhat difficult for biologists to accept a definition of life that lacks a Darwinian framework.” (Tirard et al., 2010, p. 1006)

To support the role of history, the authors quote Bernal saying that the phenomena of biology are contingent on events. But that is a statement about phenomena. Phenomena are not definitions.

“life involved another element, logically different from those occurring in physics at that time, by no means a mystical one, but an element of history. The phenomena of biology must be … contingent on events. In consequence, the unity of life is part of the history of life and, consequently, is involved in its origin” (Bernal, quoted in (Tirard et al., 2010, p. 1007))

I think it is somewhat tautological to define life in terms of Darwinian evolution. Evolution is a theory that describes the trajectories of living things over time, and their diversity. It does not define them. In my view, evolution should emerge from a theoretical definition of life; it should not be part of it. A good definition would describe a kind of physical system, and Darwinian evolution would follow as a consequence whenever such systems can reproduce, vary, and so on.

The authors also rightly insist that the basic nature of life cannot be understood without genetic material. I agree with this: we observe that living things carry within them a description of themselves, written in matter. This is how the authors enter the debate between metabolism-first and genetic-information-first scenarios, but again, this debate is about the origin, not the nature. A model of the nature of life should probably include a description of the genetic material and of how its maintenance relates to metabolism, although it would not answer the question of which came first.

Life as a phase transition

The authors also write that answers to “what is life” will come from specialists working on many different problems, from the origin of life to synthetic biology and the search for extraterrestrial life. In my opinion, these fields can feed a body of theory, but they will not give us a definition. The problem of the definition has to be tackled specifically, like any other question in science.

There is one point where I partly agree with the authors. They note that some systems exhibit some properties of life (self-organisation, replication) without being alive, and they argue that these intermediates do not abolish the two categories of living and non-living.

I would put it differently. Suppose life is circumscribed by a phase transition in the space of the parameters describing a physical system, the way water becomes ice at a given temperature. Then such a definition naturally lets systems sit on a gradient of life-likeness. The gradient may change very fast around the transition, so that most real systems fall clearly on one side or the other, but it is still a gradient. Intermediate systems are not a problem for such a definition. They are expected.

The figure below is only a cartoon of that idea, not a model of anything specific. Life-likeness \(L\) changes with some control parameter \(\theta\) as

\[L(\theta) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-(\theta - \theta_c)/w}},\]

where \(\theta_c\) is the transition point and \(w\) the width of the transition. The dashed line is the strict binary view. Make \(w\) small and the gradient becomes almost a category. Make it large and the intermediates take over.

In this picture, the interesting scientific questions are: what is \(\theta\) (or what are the parameters \(\theta\)), what physical quantities define it, where is the transition, and how sharp is it? These are questions that can, at least in principle, be answered by measurement.

What I take from it

This essay is a good map of how biologists have talked about life for two centuries. But it gives up too early. It treats the absence of a definition as a property of life, when it may simply be a property of our current theories. In physics and chemistry, empirical concepts got precise definitions once there was a theory to anchor them. I do not see why biology would be different.

So my open question is: what would a definition of life look like if we started from physics, from mass and energy flowing through a bounded system that stores information, and asked when such a system deserves to be called alive?
