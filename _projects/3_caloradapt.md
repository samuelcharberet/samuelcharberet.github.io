Metabolic rates have a long history of methodological development. In the late 19th century, Max Rubner had one dog spend 45 days in a large calorimeter to find out that the indirect and the direct methods for calorimetry gave similar values. Now, most researchers are using indirect calorimetry, which really should be called respirometry instead because it measures exchanges of gases like O<sub>2</sub> and CO<sub>2</sub>. Respirometry values are often converted to units of heat using conversion constant coefficients. However, this can be critized since the relationship between gas exchange and heat produced by organisms is not so simple {%cite kaiyala_2011 walsberg_2005 %}. Direct calorimetry has stayed the golden standard to measuring metabolic rate. This could also be critisized because heat does not tell the whole story of energy exchange and transformations. Other forms of energy that are not taken into account with this approach are mechanical work, chemical potential of molecules that leave the body of the organism. However, heat reasonably reflects non reversible energy losses that accompany most biological processes, and therefore reflects the "level of biological activity".
\\
\\
Within this project, we aim at quantifying the metabolic rate of <i>Drosopihla</i> larvae originating from two kinds of populations: adapted to poor food conditions or not. Measuring such a small heat flow is challenging, and we collaborate with Ronald Kühnlein from the Univeristy of Graz, Austria. Using a TAM IV microcalorimeter, we are able to probe powers at the scale of microwatts. By measuring growth in parallel, we are able to understand the relationship betzeen metabolic rate and growth rate within a controlled evolutive context.
