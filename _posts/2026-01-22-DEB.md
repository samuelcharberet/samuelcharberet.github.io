The Dynamic Energy Budget theory has been introduced by S.A.L.M. Kooijman in the 90'. This is a theory, which means it is a set of equations that are supposed to describe reality. Kooijman describes it in his book as <i>the best tested quantitative theory in biology</i> {% cite kooijman2009 marques2018 %}. So this it sounds like this theory should be known and used by everyone ! However, few of my colleagues actually know it and in my current lab, no one has ever published on it. But a general theory of what life is sounds like something we should try to learn and understand. This is how I started reading {% cite kooijman2009 %}. As a first note, I find a bit surprising that for a theory whith such high claims, there is no collective textbooks, but only one book written by the creator of the theory himself.

## About the name "DEB"

The name of the theory, dynamic energy budgets, might rebuke some. It is a bit complicated. In my opinion, it does not convey well what the theory does. The "dynamic" seem to refer to the fact that the model accounts for changes in the state of the organism as it grows. So here, maybe the word "ontogenic" would be adapted as well.
\\
\\
As for the "energy" part, it raises a lot of questions as to what is here defined as energy. And this might be one of the highest obstacle to the actual sucess of the theory as measured by how many people use it. Do you know of an experimentalist who works with energy ? Who, in the biological sciences research, measures the "energy" of something ? Very few people. Most people measure concentrations, masses, number of deaths, protein or RNA levels. I believe that this is because these variable became available, and the energetic aspects of living organism, are both vague and hard to measure. The use of energy in models, stems from the conservation law, which make this variable easier to track mathematically. However, there are also two other variables, for which budget equations can be written in biological processes: mass and entropy {% cite jusup_2017 %}. Among these three, mass is, by far, the easiest to measure for biological systems.

## Energy in biology

Making energy budgets is conceptually more challenging since energy that crosses the boundary of the individual can take various forms: the Gibb's free energy from nutrients that heterotrophs so love, incoming elecelectromagnetic radiation which can be harvested in the case of plants, but also generate heat or affect the chemical makeup of the individual (which in turn can affect the Gibb's free energy of chemical species in presence), but also simply sources of heat or mechanical work applied to the system. If we wanted to perform an ernegy balance at the level of one individual (or repeating meaures over several comparable individuals), we would also have to do the mass balance of the individual, because the Gibb's free energy will typically be computed using mass and mass-specific Gibbs free energy. Therefore the mass balance will always be a prerequisite to the energy balance unless we find forms of life that are made only of pure energy. The simple fact that all known life is made of stuff that have mass prompts to having a solid theory of how mass balance should work in biological systems and mshould also prompt us to choose study systems for which mass balances are experimentally easily accessible

## On the distinction between structure and reserves

Along my several years of hearing about the DEB, not having time to invest into reading about it, and then finally reading the textbook, the distinction between structure and reserve has been a culprit of much confusion. The words used there are misleading. In the language of many physiologists, the reserve refers to tissues that have high energetic content (whathever this means) and that can be used to fuel metabolism in periods of low energy availability in the environment. Therefore, many biologist think of reserve as for example, fat tissues. However, in DEB theory, a fat tissue would be almost only structure. Instead, in DEB theory, reserve is something else. Quite frankly, the definition of reserve and structure is still not clear to me, even after having read the opening chapter of the textbook, entitled "Basic concepts". I think it indicates a weakness of the exposition of the theory, or maybe of the theory itself. The sentences that most look like a definition to me is in the aforementioned chapter, given as followed: "<i>The primary difference between reserve and structure is in their dynamics: all chemical compounds in the reserve have the same turnover time, in the structure they can be different. Reserves are used to fuel all metabolic needs of the individual</i>. So reserve and structure are defined by the equality or not of their components, in turnover time. I am not sure that the naming convention of structure and reserve reflects adequately this distinction. At this point of the book, there is no equation that would enable a more precise definition: what is the unit of reserve and structure ? Energy ? Mass ? Why would turnover time matter here ? The author lets us in much confusion. This exposition is not very satisfactory, especially because the entire theory lies on this foundational distinction. Later in the textbook exposition, we are given laws that governs the rate at which reerv is mobilize etc., but still this definition is flimsy. On the epxerimental side, can we attribute any given molecule that we can measure in a biological sample to either of these categories ? How can we test the assumption that some of the components in a biological samples have equal turnover time ? The other convincing argument given by the author is that <i>the chemical composition of the individual depends on the growth rate. This can only be captured if biomass has more than one component</i>. However, the distinction could be based on another criterion.

## On the definition of maturation

There are several processes that are considered, in DEB theory, as fundamental and universal enough so as to being included in the standard DEB. Maturation is one of them. There is another one termed "maturity maintenance". Maturation is measured is energy and maturity maintenance is a flux of energy. This is also confusing to me. The reason for maintenance is, from the standpoint of non equlibrium thermodynamics, to maintain a certain state of the system that is capable of maintaining itself, reproduce, grow etc. This has an energetic cost to it and thus to stay alive (whathever this means), organisms consume energy. We can think of maturation as a process that will change the state of the system to another point in the space of microstates, such that the system is still able to maintaining itself, and grow but will gain the ability to reproduce (which is not a notion completely independant from growth itsels). However, it is not clear whether or not this would cost more than keeping the system in its original state. Out of all the states that allow maintenance, reproduction, growth, there will be some of them closer to equilibrium that others, and this is what will decide whether or not transitioning from one state to another will cost energy. Transitioning from a state of higher energy to a state of lower energy will actually release energy, not cost some. Whether or not transitioning from a "unmature" state for which reproductive systems are not ready to a "mature state" where it is will therefore not obviously cost energy. Moreover, it is not clear why maintaining this state would cost more energy than maintaining the previous one, such that there would be two distinguishable processes, one called maintenance, and one called maturity maintenance, both positive, such that a mature organism would have to pay more energy in order to maintain its state than an unmature one. It would be more sensible to me to have a process called maintenance that is an energy flux, the level of which would depend on the state of the system and which can vary in time depending on which state the system is at at time <i>t</i>.
